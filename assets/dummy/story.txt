The Clockmaker of Low Harbour

Low Harbour was the kind of town that maps forgot to name. It sat at the bottom of a steep green valley where the river finally gave up and let the sea take it, and its houses were stacked along the slope like books that had been pushed onto a shelf in a hurry. The roofs were slate, the doors were painted whatever colour had been cheapest that year, and every morning the fog came in from the water and sat on the town like a cat that refused to move. People who lived there liked it that way. People who visited usually left after a day, saying the place was lovely but somehow a little slow, as if the hours there were longer than anywhere else.

They were not wrong about the hours. In a narrow shop at the top of Ferry Lane, between a bakery and a shop that sold nothing but rope, there lived a clockmaker named Edda Vane. She was sixty-one years old, had hands that never shook, and kept every clock in Low Harbour running. The church clock, the clock above the harbour office, the small brass clocks on the mantelpieces of the fishermen's houses, the cuckoo clock in the school that the children had broken three times: all of them passed through her hands at least once a year. She cleaned them, oiled them, set them, and sent them home. And because she had done this for forty years, every clock in Low Harbour ran exactly as fast as Edda wanted it to, which was a little slower than the rest of the world.

Nobody had ever asked her to do it. It had started by accident, the winter her husband Tomas fell ill. He had been a fisherman, a big quiet man who laughed with his whole chest, and when the doctor told her he had perhaps a few months left, Edda went back to her bench and found that she could not bear the sound of the clocks around her. Every tick was a small theft. So she opened the case of the shop's long clock, the one that stood by the door, and she moved the regulator a hair's width toward slow. Then she did the same to the clock on the wall, and the one on the counter, and the little travelling clock she carried in her apron pocket. It did not make the days longer, of course. She knew that. But it made them feel longer, and that winter she needed them to feel longer more than she needed them to be right.

Tomas lived until the spring. He used to sit in the window of the shop with a blanket over his knees and watch the boats come in, and he would say that the days were beautiful and slow, and she would say yes, they were, and keep working with her back to him so he could not see her face. When he died, in April, she meant to set all the clocks right again. She took the long clock's case off and held the little key in her hand for a long time. Then she put it down and went to bed. In the morning she found that she had no wish to make the world any faster than it already was.

So the clocks of Low Harbour stayed slow. Every clock that came into the shop for cleaning went home a fraction of a minute behind, and every year the fraction grew a little. The baker opened his ovens at what his clock said was five o'clock, which was really nearer to half past. The school bell rang for the end of the day a few minutes later than the timetable said, and the children did not complain, because the extra minutes were spent outside in the yard. The ferry left the quay when the harbour clock said it should, and the ferry captain, who had a pocket watch from the city, simply learned to wait. Nobody minded very much. The town was small and far away, and the only thing that truly kept time there was the tide, and the tide did not care what anybody's clock said.

The trouble began in the autumn of Edda's sixty-second year, when a young man got off the ferry with a leather case, a long coat, and a letter from the railway company. His name was Jonah Pell, and he had been sent to build a station. The railway was coming to Low Harbour at last. It would run along the valley floor beside the river, cross the water on a bridge of iron and stone, and stop at a neat brick platform at the bottom of Ferry Lane. Trains would leave for the city four times a day. Fish could be sent to the markets while it was still cold from the sea. Visitors could arrive in the morning and be home again by night. The town council had voted for it in the spring, and now here was Jonah, twenty-six years old and very serious, with drawings under his arm and a timetable in his pocket.

The timetable was the first thing he showed the council, and the first thing that went wrong. The trains, he explained, would run on railway time, which was the same as city time, which was kept by the great observatory clock four hundred miles away and sent down the telegraph wires every morning at nine. A train that left the city at seven minutes past eight would arrive at Low Harbour at sixteen minutes past one, and leave again at twenty-five past two, and these times would not change for anybody. He said it kindly, but he said it firmly. The council members nodded. Then the mayor, a round man named Pim who owned the rope shop, took out his pocket watch, looked at it, and said that if the train arrived at sixteen past one it would be a very early lunch indeed, because it was nearly a quarter to twelve now.

Jonah looked at his own watch. It said twenty past twelve. He looked at the clock on the wall of the council room, which agreed with the mayor. He looked out of the window at the church tower, where the great clock face also agreed with the mayor. He frowned, and wrote something in his notebook, and said that he would look into it.

He looked into it for three days. He walked up and down every lane in Low Harbour with his watch in his hand, comparing it with every clock he could find. He asked the baker, the teacher, the harbour master and the woman who ran the post office what time it was, and wrote down their answers next to the time on his own watch. By the third evening he had filled eleven pages of his notebook, and every page said the same thing. Low Harbour was thirty-four minutes behind the rest of the world, and the town did not know it. Worse, the clocks did not agree with each other. The church was thirty-four minutes behind. The harbour office was thirty-one. The school was thirty-eight. The post office, which got the telegraph signal every morning, should have been right, but the postmistress set her clock by the church because she said the church was more reliable, and so the post office was thirty-four minutes behind as well. Every clock in town had drifted, slowly, in the same direction, and all of them had last been set by the same pair of hands.

On the fourth morning, Jonah climbed the steep cobbles of Ferry Lane and pushed open the door of Edda Vane's shop. A small bell rang. The shop was dim and warm and full of sound: dozens of clocks on the walls and shelves, all ticking, none of them quite together, so that the room seemed to be full of soft rain. Edda was at her bench at the back with a loupe screwed into one eye and a tiny brass wheel held in a pair of tweezers. She did not look up. She said good morning, and that she would be with him in a minute, and Jonah, who had been prepared to be stern, found himself standing very still so as not to disturb her.

It was a long minute. When she finally set the wheel down and took the loupe from her eye, she looked at him for a while without speaking, the way she looked at a clock that had come in with a problem she had not yet found. Then she said that he must be the railway man, and that she supposed he had come about the time.

Jonah admitted that he had. He put his notebook on the counter and opened it to the first page, and began to explain, carefully, what he had found. He explained about the observatory and the telegraph and railway time. He explained that a train could not wait for a town, and that if the clocks of Low Harbour said one thing and the timetable said another, then people would miss their trains, and fish would sit on the platform in the sun, and the whole point of the railway would be lost. He said it all very reasonably, and when he had finished, he asked her whether she knew why every clock in the town was slow.

Edda said that she did. She said it simply, the way she might have said that the weather was turning. Then she asked him if he would like a cup of tea, because it was a long story and the kettle was already warm.

Jonah had not planned to stay for tea. He stayed for three cups. Edda told him about Tomas, and the winter he was ill, and the long clock by the door. She told him about the morning after the funeral when she held the key in her hand and could not turn it. She told him about the forty years since, and about how the town had slowed down so gently that nobody had noticed, the way nobody notices a tide coming in until their feet are wet. She did not apologise, and she did not ask him to understand. She only told it, in order, the way she would have taken apart a clock and laid the pieces on a cloth so that he could see how they fitted together.

When she had finished, Jonah sat for a long time with his cup in his hands. The clocks ticked around them. Outside, the fog had lifted, and a thin autumn light came through the window and lay across the bench and the little brass wheel. He said, finally, that he was very sorry about her husband. Then he said that the railway was still coming, and that the clocks would still have to be put right.

Edda said she knew that too. She said she had known since the spring, when the council voted, that this day would arrive. She had only hoped it would arrive a little later. And she smiled at that, a small dry smile, and Jonah laughed despite himself, and something in the room changed. He asked her whether she would help him. She said that she would think about it.

She thought about it for a week. During that week Jonah went back to his work. He walked the valley with a team of surveyors, marking the line of the track with little wooden stakes. He met with the quarry owners about the stone for the bridge. He wrote letters to the city, long ones, explaining that there was a small difficulty with the local time and that he expected to resolve it shortly. And every evening, when the light went, he found that his feet carried him up Ferry Lane, and that he stood for a minute outside the clock shop, listening to the soft rain of ticking through the door, before he went back down the hill to his room above the inn.

On the seventh evening, the door opened while he was standing there. Edda looked at him, unsurprised, and told him to come in. She had made a decision, she said, but it was not quite the one he wanted. She would set the clocks right. Every one of them, the church and the school and the harbour office and the little brass clocks on the mantelpieces. But she would not do it all at once. If she turned every clock in Low Harbour forward by thirty-four minutes in a single night, she said, the town would wake up and find that half an hour of its life had vanished, and nobody would know where it had gone. The baker would burn his bread. The children would be late for school and not understand why. The old men who sat on the harbour wall every afternoon would find the afternoon shorter and feel cheated without knowing what had been taken. She would not do that to them. She had slowed the town down gently, and she would speed it up gently, a minute at a time.

Jonah did the arithmetic in his head. Thirty-four minutes, a minute at a time, would take more than a month if she did it every day. The railway was due to open in the spring. He said that there was time, and Edda nodded, and said that there was always more time than people thought, and that this was rather the point.

So they began. Every morning, before the town woke, Edda went out with her bag of tools and her little brass key, and she visited one or two of the town's clocks and moved them forward by a single minute. She started with the church, because the church was the clock the others followed, and climbing the tower stairs in the dark took her a long time, and Jonah, without being asked, began to meet her at the bottom of the tower and carry her bag. She did not thank him, exactly, but the next morning there was a second cup in her basket, and a flask of tea, and they drank it together on the narrow platform behind the great clock face while the sky over the sea went from black to grey to pale gold.

The town noticed nothing at first. A minute a day is a very small thing. The baker found that his bread was ready a little earlier than he expected and was pleased. The ferry captain, comparing his pocket watch with the harbour clock, raised an eyebrow and said nothing. The children at the school did not notice that the bell rang a minute sooner each week, because children do not count minutes, they count the time until something good happens, and that never changes. Only the old men on the harbour wall noticed, in their way. One of them, a man named Haskel who had been at sea for fifty years and was now mostly made of salt and opinions, said that the afternoons were getting shorter. The others told him it was autumn and the afternoons always got shorter. Haskel said that this was a different kind of shorter. Nobody listened to him, which was usual.

Meanwhile the railway grew. The wooden stakes became a long raw scar of earth along the valley floor, and then a bed of crushed stone, and then two lines of iron that caught the morning light. The bridge rose out of the river one arch at a time. The station at the bottom of Ferry Lane went from a hole in the ground to a set of brick walls to a building with a roof and a clock of its own, a big plain railway clock with black hands, which Jonah set by the telegraph signal every morning and which was, as a result, thirty-one minutes ahead of the church, then twenty-five, then nineteen.

It was the station clock that finally gave the game away. People walking down Ferry Lane began to look from the church tower to the new clock and back again, and frown, and ask each other which one was right. The postmistress, who had always set her clock by the church, came to the station one morning and asked Jonah, quite sharply, why his clock was wrong. He told her, quite gently, that it was not. She looked at the telegraph tape he showed her for a long time. Then she walked back up the hill and stood outside the clock shop with her arms folded until Edda came to the door.

By the evening the whole town knew. Low Harbour had been running slow for forty years, and Edda Vane had done it, and Edda Vane was now undoing it, a minute a day, with the help of the railway man. The news went from the post office to the bakery to the inn to the harbour wall, and it grew a little at every stop, the way news does in small towns. By the time it reached Haskel it had become a story in which Edda had stolen half an hour from every person in the town and hidden it somewhere, and the railway man had come to make her give it back. Haskel said that he had told them so. For once, the others listened.

There was a meeting at the council room that Friday. Nearly everybody came. The room was too small, so people stood in the doorway and in the corridor and on the steps outside, and the windows were opened so that the ones in the street could hear. Mayor Pim stood at the front and banged a small wooden hammer on the table until the room was quiet. Then he asked Edda, who was sitting in the front row with her hands folded in her lap, whether it was true.

Edda stood up. She was not a tall woman, and the room was full of people who had known her their whole lives, and for a moment she looked very small. Then she told them. She told it the way she had told it to Jonah, in order, without apology, laying the pieces out on the cloth. She told them about Tomas and the winter and the key. She told them that she had not meant to keep the town slow, only that she had never found a morning when she wanted to make it fast. And she told them that she was sorry, not for the minutes, but for not asking them first. The minutes, she said, had been hers to give. But the town had not been hers to give them to.

The room was very quiet when she sat down. Then somebody near the back laughed, a short surprised laugh, and said that this explained why their grandmother had always said Low Harbour was the only place where a person could get a full day's work done and still have the afternoon left over. Somebody else said that it explained why visitors always looked so tired, and that perhaps they were not tired at all but simply thirty-four minutes ahead of everyone else. Then the baker stood up and said, a little stiffly, that he had always been proud that his bread was the first in the valley to come out of the oven, and that he now understood he had been getting up half an hour later than he thought for most of his life, and that frankly he did not see what there was to complain about.

That broke the room open. People began to talk all at once. Some of them were angry, the way people are when they find out a thing has been done to them without their knowing, even a gentle thing. Some of them were delighted, the way people are when they find out their town has a secret. A few were sad, because they had loved Tomas too, and had not known how much Edda had missed him. The teacher said that the school timetable would have to be rewritten. The harbour master said that the tide tables had always been right, so at least nobody had drowned. Mayor Pim banged his hammer and nobody paid any attention to it.

In the end it was Haskel who settled it. He stood up slowly, holding the back of the chair in front of him, and waited until the room noticed him, which took a while. Then he said that he did not much care what the clocks said and never had. He said that at sea you told the time by the light and the tide and your own stomach, and that the only clock that had ever mattered to him was the one that told him when to come home. He said that the railway would come whether they liked it or not, and that it was probably a good thing, because his granddaughter lived in the city and he had not seen her for four years. And he said that if Edda wanted to give the town back its minutes slowly, so that nobody noticed them going, then he for one thought that was a kindness, and that there had not been so many kindnesses in his life that he could afford to turn one down. Then he sat down, and the room was quiet again, and the vote, when Mayor Pim finally managed to call it, was very nearly unanimous. The clocks would be put right, one minute a day, by Edda Vane, until the railway opened.

After that, things were different on the morning rounds. People began to wait for her. The baker left a small loaf on the step of the church tower every morning, still warm, wrapped in a cloth. The children at the school asked if they could watch her set the bell clock, and she let them, two at a time, standing on a bench to see inside the case while she explained what each wheel did. The postmistress, who had been the angriest at the meeting, came to the shop one evening with a pie and said that she supposed it was not the worst thing anyone had ever done to her, and stayed for an hour. And the old men on the harbour wall began to keep a tally, scratched in chalk on the stones, of how many minutes were left. Haskel did the scratching. He said it was the most useful thing he had done in years.

Jonah kept carrying the bag up the church tower stairs, even though half the town now offered to do it. On the platform behind the clock face, with the tea and the baker's bread, Edda told him things she had not told anyone. She told him how Tomas had proposed to her in a rowing boat in the rain and then dropped the ring in the bottom of the boat and spent twenty minutes finding it among the fish scales. She told him about the year the harbour froze. She told him how she had learned the trade from an old clockmaker in the city who had been almost blind, and who had taught her to listen to a clock rather than look at it, because a clock that was about to fail always changed its voice first. And Jonah, who had not meant to tell anyone anything, told her about his father, who had worked on the railways his whole life and had died the year before, and how Jonah had taken this job partly because it was as far away from the city as the line could go, and partly because his father had always said that the best thing a railway could do was bring somewhere lonely a little closer to everywhere else.

The minutes went by, one a day. Thirty became twenty, and twenty became ten. The chalk tally on the harbour wall grew long, and people stopped to read it on their way past, the way they might stop to read the weather. The bridge was finished in the first week of the new year, and the first test engine crossed it with its whistle screaming and half the town lined up along the riverbank cheering, though most of them could not have said exactly what they were cheering for. The station clock and the church clock crept closer together until they were only a few minutes apart, and then only one or two, and people began to say that they could hardly tell the difference any more, which was exactly what Edda had intended.

There was one clock left at the end. It was the long clock by the door of Edda's shop, the first one she had ever slowed, the one Tomas had sat beside in the window that winter. She had saved it for last without ever quite deciding to. On the morning before the railway opened, when every other clock in Low Harbour was right to the second, she stood in front of it with the little brass key in her hand, exactly as she had stood forty years before, and found that her hand did not want to move.

Jonah was there. He had come up the hill without the bag, because there was nothing left to carry. He did not say anything. He only stood beside her and listened, the way she had taught him to, to the slow deep tick of the long clock, which was thirty-four minutes behind the world and always had been, and which sounded, he thought, like somebody breathing in their sleep.

After a while Edda said that she had been thinking about what Haskel said at the meeting. About the only clock that mattered being the one that told you when to come home. She said that she had spent forty years making the days feel longer because she was afraid of how few of them there were. And she said that she had only now, this winter, with the tea on the church tower and the children on the bench and the bread on the step, understood that the days had never been short. They had only been empty. She had filled them with slowness because she did not know what else to fill them with. And now she did.

Then she opened the case of the long clock, and fitted the key, and turned it, and the hands swept forward thirty-four minutes in a single smooth movement, and the clock struck nine, a little late, and then fell into step with every other clock in Low Harbour. The sound of the shop changed. For the first time in forty years, all the clocks on the walls and the shelves were ticking together, and the soft rain became something else, something steadier, like footsteps. Edda closed the case and put the key in her apron pocket and said that she supposed that was that.

The railway opened the next morning at sixteen minutes past one, exactly, by every clock in town. The whole of Low Harbour came down to the platform to watch the first train arrive. The children had made flags. The baker had made a cake in the shape of an engine, which collapsed slightly in the sun but was eaten anyway. Mayor Pim gave a speech that went on too long, and banged his little wooden hammer on the railing of the platform at the end of it for no reason anybody could see. And when the train came round the last bend of the valley and slowed into the station with a great sigh of steam, the first person off it was a young woman with a small suitcase, who looked up and down the crowded platform until she saw an old man at the back, leaning on a stick, made mostly of salt and opinions, and ran to him.

Haskel did not say anything for a long time. He held his granddaughter and looked over her shoulder at the hills, and the people nearest to him looked away to give him room. Later, on the harbour wall, he took the chalk out of his pocket and rubbed out the long tally of minutes with the side of his hand, and wrote instead, in big uneven letters, that the train had come on time.

Jonah stayed in Low Harbour for another month, to see that everything was working properly, and then for another, because there were always small things that needed fixing, and then, somehow, for good. The railway company made him station master. He set the big plain clock on the platform by the telegraph every morning at nine, and every morning at ten past nine, without fail, he walked up Ferry Lane to the clock shop, where there would be tea, and the soft steady sound of a room full of clocks that all agreed with each other, and an old woman at a bench at the back with a loupe in her eye and a tiny wheel in her tweezers, who would say good morning, and that she would be with him in a minute.

It was always a long minute. Neither of them minded. Some things, Edda said once, are worth being a little slow for, as long as you are not slow on purpose to keep the world away. Jonah wrote that down in his notebook, on the twelfth page, after the eleven pages of times and clocks and all the minutes that the town had lost and found again. It was the last thing he ever wrote in it. He kept the notebook in the drawer of the station office for the rest of his life, and when people asked him what it was, he said it was the record of the only time he had ever been asked to make a town faster, and had found out that what it really needed was to be a little less alone.

And the clocks of Low Harbour kept perfect time after that, more or less. Visitors still said the town was lovely but somehow slow. They were wrong now, about the hours. But they were not wrong about the town. It took its time, the way the tide did, the way a good clock does, and everybody who lived there agreed that this was the right speed for it.
